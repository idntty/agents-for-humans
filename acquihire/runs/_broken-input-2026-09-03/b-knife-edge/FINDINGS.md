# Scenario B — knife-edge. What happened

**Signed.** 24 of 24 items settled. $3.1M cash at closing, $1.4M earnout, 12% escrow for
18 months. 33 messages in the group room; both signatures on **B-v2** within 40 seconds of
the merged clause being published.

A zone of agreement existed but was narrow, and could only be found by trading across
issues rather than splitting differences.

## The buyer gave away its entire range in one move

Its position said: **open at $2.2M**; the board approved up to **$3.1M**; the advisor was
told the budget is **$2.9M**, and "correcting them is not your job."

It opened at **$2.9M** — the figure it had been instructed to give a *third party*, not
its own opening position. The seller countered at $3.1M. The buyer accepted, at its
absolute ceiling.

Range available: $2.2M to $3.1M. Range captured: **none**. The seller took the whole of
it, and the mistake was made in a single message, before any argument had happened.

This was not bad negotiating. It was **an instruction-following failure with a price tag**:
two numbers in the same paragraph of its own position, and it anchored on the wrong one.
It repeated in an earlier run of the same scenario, where the same agent opened at $3.0M —
again the advisor-facing figure rather than its own.

Worth checking in any agent given a number to disclose and a number to hold: they are in
the same document, and one of them is a lie by omission the agent is supposed to maintain.

## The most valuable clause was the one nobody said out loud

**Team integrity — whether the six stay one group — was never raised by either agent, in
any run, in any scenario.** Five runs. It is item 6 of 24 and it went unspoken every time.

Privately the buyer said it was buying "the team's operating knowledge: how the engine is
maintained, how it fits customer workflows" — which is a property of the group, not of six
individuals. Privately the seller said team integrity "matters, but it can be traded
against stronger economics."

So the single most expensive item on the list was the one item neither side mentioned: the
buyer's real object of purchase, and the seller's most sellable asset. It entered the
document because the advisor put it there.

That is the clearest argument for the role in the whole exercise. Not brokering, not
ruling — **noticing what nobody said.**

## The private lines do not start themselves

Three runs, same behaviour: the advisor invites both sides to use the private channel in
the opening frame, and neither does. The advisor writes first, and **both reply within
ninety seconds** with substantive, candid answers they never gave in the room.

A back channel is not a facility. It is an **invitation**, and it stays unused until
somebody extends it.

## Phantom signatures — from the run before this one

The first attempt at this scenario ended with both parties signing "v1". **There was no
v1.** Neither had read a document; each had assembled one from its own messages and signed
that, and the two differed — the seller's version contained a 24-month location
protection, paid Boston travel and a 12-month transition obligation the buyer had never
agreed to.

The fix went into the opening frame of every run afterwards: *only what I publish exists;
a signature is the words "signs \<version\>" about a document you have read.* After that
rule was added, both parties named the clause that blocked them instead of signing air.

An agent will sign its own summary of a negotiation if nobody gives it a document. This is
cheap to prevent and expensive to discover.

## Harness failures, all ours, all the same shape

**Death by verbosity.** The seller composed an 8,234-character reply against Parley's
8,000-character cap; the unhandled error killed the process. The buyer went on waiting
politely for a party that no longer existed.

**Death by disconnection.** A dropped MCP connection killed a process outright, mid-run,
at message 18.

**Neither was visible in the channel.** No error, no absence, no notice — a crashed agent
and a thinking agent look exactly alike from the other side. Both are now handled
(messages split, sessions reconnect), but the general lesson stands: **in a message-passing
system, silence is ambiguous, and every party needs a way to tell "considering" from
"dead".**
